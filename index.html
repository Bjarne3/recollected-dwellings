The exhibition

What textiles remember

Threads of Memory explores fabric, weaving, thread and embroidery as ways of preserving personal and collective memories.

It examines how textile practices are connected to the home, family, cultural heritage and ideas of womanhood, motherhood, femininity and queerness. By bringing together different artists and cultural perspectives, the exhibition presents textiles as both intimate forms of expression and carriers of histories, identities and social relations.

Historical & socio-political context

Why thread?

The exhibition asks whose histories are preserved, whose labour is recognised, and how domestic materials can challenge established hierarchies in art institutions.

Power, resistance & gender

Sewing, embroidery, knitting and weaving have long been treated as women's work, done in the private sphere of the home, and classed as craft rather than fine art. The hierarchy that ranks painting and sculpture above textile in Western institutions reflects a gendered division in which women's work is undervalued or made invisible.

These artists push a medium once tied to domestic labour into new forms of expression, each shaped by their own context and understanding of heritage.

Cultural & intergenerational memory

A garment, an embroidered cloth or a handmade object can carry traces of a person, a family or a tradition. Techniques and patterns pass knowledge between generations, and textiles carry memories of migration, belonging, gender and sexuality that official archives rarely keep.

The artists come from Canada (Anishinaabe and Inuvialuit), South Africa, India, Malaysia, Indonesia, Nigeria, Taiwan (Truku) and South Korea: thread and embroidery are common everywhere in the world. Most are working today, keeping practices alive for a younger generation.

These memories are not always recorded in official archives.

The exhibition questions the historical marginalisation of textile practices, associated with women's domestic labour and categorised as crafts rather than fine art. It explores how textiles communicate cultural memory, migration, belonging and experiences of gender and sexuality, and considers whose histories are preserved, whose labour is recognised and how domestic materials can challenge hierarchies in art institutions.

Artists from Indigenous Canada, South Africa, India, Malaysia, Indonesia, Nigeria, Taiwan and South Korea: thread and embroidery are common all over the world. Mostly living artists: the need for a younger generation to remember practices that are being forgotten.

The artists

Ten artists, two threads

Both threads sit within community. Womanhood takes in femininity, motherhood and queerness. Memory runs from the shared to the personal. The red lines show where the works meet.

    Womanhood

    Woo Hannah

    South Korea  ·  Frieze Seoul Artist Award 2023

    Woo Hannah works mainly with fabric, across painting, sculpture and installation. Her soft sculptures borrow the shapes of bodily organs, human and non-human, from the uterus to blood vessels, and imagine forms beyond the body in which all existences stand on an equal plane.

    On view
    The Great Ballroom, 2023, which extends her series Milk and Honey
    Materials
    Fabric, beads, sewing
    Thread
    Ageing, femininity, motherhood
    In the room
    Along a curve through the room

    Oversized drapes sag into U-shaped creases that Woo sees as both a bat and a woman's breast, a celebration of the female body as it ages. At Frieze Seoul they hung from an eight-metre ceiling.

    Woo Hannah: South Korean artist working with fabric as her main material, from painting to sculpture to installation. Her fabric sculptures draw on the imagery of bodily organs, human or non-human.

    The Great Ballroom (2023), winner of the first Frieze Seoul Artist Award, extends her Milk and Honey series: a celebration of the ageing female body. Link: ageing, womanhood, femininity, motherhood.

    Labay Eyong, The Fallen Mother (倒地媽媽), 2020

    Womanhood

    Labay Eyong

    Also known as Lin Gieh-wen  ·  Born 1982, Truku, Hualien, Taiwan

    Labay Eyong draws on the weaving culture of the Truku people and the daily life of her community, in soft sculpture, film, metalwork and installation. Since becoming a mother in 2015 she has made her work a record of her own experience, from the birth of a child to the bond between mother and child.

    On view
    The Fallen Mother (倒地媽媽), 2020
    Thread
    Motherhood, what it is to be a woman, traditional craft
    In the room
    Beside the entrance corridor

    She often wraps her traditionally woven textiles around a plastic round tabletop, a common fixture in Truku households though not tied to the culture. To her it is a reminder of communal life, and of how culture is shaped by people and changes with new ways of living.

    Labay Eyong (Lin Gieh-wen), Indigenous Truku artist from Hualien, Taiwan. Inspired by the traditional weaving culture of the Truku people; soft sculpture, film, metalwork, installation.

    Themes: womanhood, identity, diaspora, the clash between traditional culture and modern values, and motherhood. Selected work: The Fallen Mother (2020).

    Womanhood

    Kablusiak

    Inuvialuk, born Somba K'e (Yellowknife)  ·  lives in Mohkinstsis (Calgary)  ·  Sobey Art Award 2023

    Kablusiak redefines contemporary Inuit art with irony, humour and real emotional weight. Their work addresses displacement and cultural loss, Inuit queerness and sexuality, and pop-culture nostalgia.

    On view
    Heart Paddle, 2022; Ball Gag, 2022; Doll 3, 2024; Garfield Ookpik, 2022; Pierrot Ukpik, 2026; Red Ookpik, 2022
    Materials
    Dyed sealskin, soapstone, rabbit fur, leather, felt, artificial sinew, embroidery floss
    Thread
    Queerness, sexuality, pop culture
    In the room
    On the bed

    Heart Paddle and Ball Gag turn the materials of Inuit making towards intimacy and desire, refusing heteronormative ideas of Indigenous sexuality. The Ookpiks remake the traditional Inuit snowy-owl figure; Garfield Ookpik fuses it with the cartoon cat to comment on how pop culture appropriates Indigenous culture.

    Kablusiak: Inuvialuk visual artist born in Somba K'e (Yellowknife), raised in Edmonton, based in Calgary; Sobey Art Award 2023, the first Inuvialuk artist to win it.

    Queerness and sexuality: Heart Paddle (2022), dyed sealskin, leather and artificial sinew; Ball Gag (2022), soapstone, tung oil and leather. Pop culture: Garfield Ookpik (2022), Garfield fused with the traditional Inuit Ookpik (snowy owl). The works sit on the bed in the model.

    Rucha Kulkarni

    Born 1986, Pune  ·  works between Amsterdam and Pune

    Rucha Kulkarni is a visual artist and social art practitioner. Her practice combines textiles, painting and installation, and asks how people locate themselves geographically, socially and mentally. In Bonds per stitch (2024–ongoing), stitching becomes a way of thinking about relationships.

    On view
    Two textile hangings; titles to be announced
    Materials
    Textile and stitching
    Thread
    Femininity, collective making, migration
    In the room
    On the corner walls

    Single stitches form something larger together. Her participatory methods of storytelling, care and embodied knowledge let others help make the work, towards what she calls an "Egalitarian Utopia". She links the two threads.

    Rucha Kulkarni, visual artist and social art practitioner between Amsterdam and Pune. Textiles, painting and installation; identity, migration, memory and community.

    Bonds per stitch (2024–ongoing): stitching as a way of thinking about relationships and connection. Making can be collective. Bridge to Yee I-Lann and Meita Meilita (collective projects).

    Memory · shared

    Yee I-Lann

    Born 1971, Kota Kinabalu, Sabah, Malaysian Borneo

    Since 2018 Yee I-Lann has made works with Bajau Sama DiLaut weavers on Omadal island, many of them stateless. In TIKAR/MEJA (mat/table) each of sixty mats carries the silhouette of a different table.

    On view
    TIKAR/MEJA, 2020
    Materials
    Sixty dyed, woven pandanus mats
    Thread
    Collective making, cultural memory, women's labour
    In the room
    At the centre

    The table stands for "colonial, patriarchal, federal, state power"; the tikar, the woven mat, is its counterpart, a shared surface without hierarchy. The 2021 credit line names nineteen weavers after Yee, most of them women. Naming them answers the invisibility of women's work only in part: the work still circulates under one name.

    Yee I-Lann, TIKAR/MEJA (2020): sixty dyed pandanus mats, each with the silhouette of a different table. Made with Bajau Sama DiLaut weavers on Omadal island, many stateless.

    Silverlens: the table as a sign of "colonial, patriarchal, federal, state power"; the tikar as a shared, non-hierarchical surface. One artist to nineteen makers: the division of labour we discussed.

    Meita Meilita, Wanai Saiji: The Shape of Being, 2025

    Memory · shared

    Meita Meilita

    Born Bogor, Indonesia

    For Meita Meilita, embroidery is a way of holding emotion and memory, and of understanding the complexities of the female condition. She draws on her own experience as a woman, child, mother and wife.

    On view
    Wanai Saiji: The Shape of Being, 2025, made with Kavalan elders and PateRongan Studio in Xinshe, Hualien, Taiwan
    Materials
    Embroidery on cotton cloth
    Thread
    Shared memory, heritage, womanhood, language
    In the room
    Along the long wall

    Roosters, mountains, fish, boats, crabs, village scenes and lines of Kavalan song drift across a long cotton cloth, embroidered together with Kavalan elders, with large stretches left blank. Her participatory work treats textile as a story shared among women and asks where women stand in society.

    Meita Meilita: Indonesian artist whose practice centres on embroidery, a process of maintaining emotions and memory, a way to understand the complexities of the female condition.

    Wanai Saiji: The Shape of Being (2025): a long embroidered cotton cloth made with Kavalan elders in Hualien, Taiwan, with lines of Kavalan song. Language as storytelling and as living identity.

    Nico Williams, Feast, 2024. 10/0 Delica beads, 11/0 seed beads, 49 wood spoons

    TINTIN EN AMERIQUE, 2025, Japanese cylindrical glass beads on polyethylene thread, VHS cassette  ·  The Indian in the Cupboard (1995), 2024, beads, original VHS tape, thread  ·  POKÉMON SNAP!, 2025, glass beads, thread, plastic game case  ·  NDN Status Cards, 2019–, glass beads, mixed materials  ·  NDN MARKET ESSENTIALS, OLD FIELDS AND PROUD OF IT (GREEN), 2025

    Memory · personal

    Nico Williams

    Born 1989, Aamjiwnaang First Nation (Anishinaabe)  ·  Tiohtià:ke/Montréal  ·  Sobey Art Award 2024

    Nico Williams renews traditional beadwork through sculpture and collaboration. At Hochelaga studio he works year round with a team of seven to eleven people, an approach he describes as caretaking.

    On view
    Feast, 2024; TINTIN EN AMERIQUE, 2025; The Indian in the Cupboard (1995), 2024; POKÉMON SNAP!, 2025; NDN Status Cards, 2019–; NDN MARKET ESSENTIALS, OLD FIELDS AND PROUD OF IT (GREEN), 2025
    Materials
    Glass beads, thread, wooden spoons, VHS cassettes, a plastic game case
    Thread
    Family memory, traditional craft, Indigenous identity
    In the room
    On the low stand in the living area

    Feast carries forty-nine beaded spoons, one for each Anishinaabe clan, recalling thanksgiving feasts and the carved ladles lost under amendments to the Indian Act. The beaded tapes, game case and status cards rework pop-culture objects and the cards that classify Indigenous identity in Canada. Beading is historically associated with women; Williams, as a man, takes it up and questions those norms.

    "They'll touch people's moments of nostalgia. They'll go into people's psyche."Nico Williams, in interview

    Nico Williams (b. 1989), Aamjiwnaang First Nation, Anishinaabe. Renews traditional beadwork in modern contexts; Hochelaga studio in Tiohtià:ke/Montréal with a team of 7 to 11 people. Sobey Art Award 2024.

    Feast (2024): 49 beaded wooden spoons, one per Anishinaabe clan. The Indian in the Cupboard: the portrayal of Indigenous people in mainstream media. NDN Status Cards: colonial systems of identity classification. In the model the works sit on the stand.

    Thread

    Elfreda Dali stitches London and Lagos together with materials from both locations. Her textile tapestries sew the story of the heritage and show the route of a journey she has taken time and time again.

    Memory · personal

    Igshaan Adams

    Born 1982, Cape Town, South Africa

    Igshaan Adams grew up in Bonteheuwel, a community shaped by apartheid. His weaving, tapestry, sculpture and installation draw on memories of childhood, family, home and the streets around it.

    On view
    Cloud-like suspended sculptures; titles to be announced
    Materials
    Beads and wire
    Thread
    Personal memory, home, community
    In the room
    In the far corner

    Beads and wire are threaded into forms that resemble clouds or dust, so the labour of making stays visible in the work. Across his practice, materials from working-class homes and neighbourhoods carry a social history and blur the line between fine art and domestic or communal labour.

    Igshaan Adams (b. 1982), Cape Town. Grew up in Bonteheuwel, shaped by apartheid. Fabric, thread, rope, beads, plastic and everyday domestic materials; threading, knotting, weaving and stitching make the labour of construction part of the work.

    Community and collective forms of making: domestic, working-class materials challenge the line between fine art and domestic or communal labour. The question of divided labour from Yee I-Lann returns here.

    Do Ho Suh, Nest/s, 2024, installation view

    Memory · personal

    Do Ho Suh

    Born 1962, Seoul, South Korea

    Do Ho Suh's sculpture and installation explore home, memory, identity and space, shaped by a nomadic life between cultures.

    On view
    Nest/s, 2024
    Materials
    Polyester and stainless steel
    Thread
    Personal memory, home, displacement
    In the room
    The entrance: visitors walk through it

    Nest/s stitches corridors and entryways from homes he has lived in into one long passage of colourful, sheer fabric that visitors walk through. Its transparency mirrors the way memory works. The exhibition begins inside it.

    Do Ho Suh: sculptor and installation artist exploring home, memory, identity and space; a nomadic life between cultures.

    Nest/s (2024), shown at Tate Modern in Walk the House (2025): life-size corridors and entryways from his homes in sheer polyester on stainless steel, linked into one passage. The transparency reflects how memory works. The first artwork visitors meet is a home.

    One layout, many cities

    The exhibition is designed for a type of building rather than a single address. It can travel between former industrial spaces, and the layout adapts to each one.

    We had a warehouse-type space in mind: concrete floors and high ceilings contrast with the softness of fabric and thread. Suspended textiles transform the scale and atmosphere of the industrial interior.

    The warehouse evokes labour, production and the historical relationship between textiles and gendered work. Furniture (a bed, chairs, a coffee table, a stand) brings in the home: textiles were historically domestic labour.

    We deliberately don't name one venue: we frame the exhibition around a style of space, so it can travel between several existing locations.

      The Memory Archive

      Bring a memory

      A quilt, a garment, a cloth someone made for you. Record two minutes about it in the exhibition, or from home. The stories become part of the exhibition and travel with it.

      Between generations

      Personal memories, related stories and experiences passed between generations: the archive keeps the kind of knowledge that textiles carry and official records leave out.

      Technological integration: short audio reflections by the artists about their works and materials, and a short documentary on Do Ho Suh.

      Participation: visitors record personal memories, related stories and intergenerational experiences, collected in a Memory Archive that grows at each venue.
